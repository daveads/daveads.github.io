When I think about raising money, I also think about the conversation that comes after it.

A year after someone backs me, 

- what would they say about working with me? 
- Would they understand where their money went? 
- Would they know what we had built, what we had learned, and where we were struggling? 
- Would they feel that I had taken their belief in me seriously?

Those questions matter to me.

I live in Nigeria. Building a company from here means working through constraints, including distance from many of the people I hope to build relationships with. There are fewer opportunities for someone on the other side of the world to meet me casually or watch how I work over time.

That makes me think carefully about how I make my work visible, explain my decisions, and earn trust.

I want to build relationships that last for years. I would like the people who back me to know enough about my character and my work that, when I bring them another difficult problem worth solving, they are willing to listen.

That has to be earned through what I do with the opportunity in front of me.

For me, keeping my word starts with being careful about what I promise. If I say an investment will help us reach a particular milestone, I should be able to explain the work involved, the assumptions behind the plan, and how we will measure progress. I should understand the terms I agree to and take those commitments seriously.

Once the money arrives, those commitments should remain visible in how I run the company.

I want to be able to explain why we hired someone, why we spent money on a particular problem, and what changed as a result. Where an expense or experiment fails to produce what we expected, I want to understand why and make a better decision next time.

Communication matters just as much when the news is difficult. A delayed product, a customer who walks away, or an assumption that turns out to be wrong should lead to an honest conversation. People who have backed me should have enough information to understand the situation while there is still time to respond.

Building also requires changing your mind. Research can challenge a plan. Customers can reveal a different problem. When that happens, I want to explain the evidence, discuss the consequences, and agree on any necessary changes to our commitments.

I cannot promise that every decision will work. I can commit to doing the work, taking responsibility for my decisions, and being truthful about the results.

My focus now is Merdra. I want to build infrastructure that allows medical information to follow a patient across healthcare providers and become useful to the software built around their care.

My vision for Merdra is ambitious. Software is our focus today, and over time I want us to expand into hardware that works with the infrastructure we are building. Getting there will take sustained work. I want us to earn each next step through what we build, learn, and deliver today.

A lasting investor relationship will still require each new idea to stand on its own merits. What I hope carries forward is a clear record of how I work.

Years from now, I would like someone introducing me to be able to say: he takes his commitments seriously, he tells you what is happening, and he keeps doing the difficult work.

I want to earn that description through the way I build [Merdra](https://www.merdra.com/)
